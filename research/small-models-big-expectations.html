Small Models, Big Expectations: Notes on Local Inference in 2026

October 24, 2026

Our first research note starts with an admission. We tried running agent work on small local models, and it didn't hold up. Local inference isn't a dead end, but the gap between running a model and running an agent on that model is wider than the hype suggests.

What we tried

Small open weight models in the 3B to 9B range, quantized to fit entry level consumer hardware, used as the core of a working assistant: everyday tasks, document handling, research summaries, and tool calls into real services.

What broke

Memory is the ceiling. A quantized 8B model fits. The context it needs does not. An agent's working memory (instructions, tool definitions, history, retrieved documents) grows fast, and the cache that holds it grows with it. Most local setups top out somewhere between 8K and 32K tokens of usable context. A real agent burns through that quickly.

Single calls are easy. Chains are hard. Small models can pick the right tool for one request reasonably well. Docker's evaluation of 21 models found Qwen3 8B close to hosted models on tool selection. But agents don't make one call. They make dozens in a row, and small errors compound. Multistep planning across many tools is still where small models drift.

Accuracy costs time. The most accurate local models in that same evaluation were also among the slowest. Qwen3 14B averaged around 142 seconds per interaction. Hosted models answered in a few seconds.

Security costs tokens. Hardening an agent against prompt injection means longer instructions, provenance rules, and confirmation steps. Every guardrail eats context a small model can't spare.

Where local inference actually stands

Small models have improved fast. Phi-4 mini, Qwen3 4B, Gemma 3 4B, and Llama 3.2 3B each fit in a few gigabytes and handle single turn tool use. Researchers at NVIDIA have argued that small models should take on most routine steps in agent systems and hand the hard parts to larger ones. Local runtimes keep getting faster.

The honest summary for late 2026:

Small local models are good at narrow, well defined jobs: classification, extraction, formatting, single tool calls, offline drafting.

They are not yet good at being the whole agent: long context, multistep planning, and holding a complex rule set over a long session.

The real constraint is memory, not compute. In our view, practical local agents start around 24GB of dedicated GPU memory, and serious ones need 48GB or more.

What's next for the lab

Split the work. Small local models handle narrow, private steps. Larger models handle planning and synthesis.

Move up a hardware tier. Our next round of testing runs on dedicated GPU hardware with enough memory for 30B to 70B class open weight models at usable context lengths.

Measure instead of guess. Future notes will publish our own numbers: tool call accuracy over long chains, real context limits, and cost per task against hosted equivalents.

The goal isn't local for its own sake. It's knowing exactly which parts of a workflow can stay private on hardware you own, and which can't yet.
